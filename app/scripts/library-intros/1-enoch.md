The Books of Enoch—primarily the Ethiopic 1 Enoch preserved in full—stand as one of the most profound and misunderstood treasures of ancient inspired literature. Attributed to Enoch, the seventh from Adam, who “walked with Elohim (God), and he was not, for Elohim (God) took him” (Genesis 5:24), this text unveils visions of the heavens, the fall of the watchers, the secrets of creation, astronomical order, dream prophecies, and eschatological judgment. Composed in stages between the 3rd century BCE and the 1st century CE, it circulated widely among the righteous in the Second Temple period, influencing the Dead Sea Scrolls community and echoing directly in the Renewed Covenant.

The Epistle of Yahudah (Jude) quotes Enoch explicitly: “And Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied about these men also, saying, ‘Behold, Yahuah (God) comes with ten thousands of His set-apart ones, to execute judgment on all, to convict all who are wicked of all their wicked works which they have done in a wicked way, and of all the harsh things which wicked sinners have spoken against Him’” (Jude 1:14-15, cf. Enoch 1:9). This is no mere allusion—Yahudah (Judah) treats Enoch’s words as authoritative prophecy. Hebrews 11:5 praises Enoch’s faith and translation, while other New Testament passages (e.g., 2 Peter 2:4 on angels in chains, Revelation’s apocalyptic imagery) bear clear marks of Enochian influence. Far from irrelevant, these writings illuminate the canonical narrative, filling in details about the sons of Elohim (Genesis 6:1-4), the pre-flood world, and the coming Day of Yahuah (God).

Yet lies have clouded this light for centuries. Some falsely claim Enoch portrays Enoch himself as the Messiah—a distortion nowhere supported in the text. Enoch is the righteous scribe and visionary who beholds the Son of Man and the Elect One (Enoch 46–48, 71), pointing forward to the true Messiah (Christ), not claiming the role. Others twist Enoch’s 364-day solar calendar (detailed in chapters 72–82) into endorsement of a “false” system, ignoring how it aligns with Yahuah’s ordained feasts, sabbaths, and creation order (cf. Jubilees 6; Genesis 1). Mainstream traditions dismiss Enoch as non-canonical fringe, stripping its prophetic weight to maintain control over interpretation. We reject these deceptions. And we read this text as Scripture reads itself—as the account of the seed of promise, the Adamic line and their history with Yahuah (God). It is not a chronicle of all humanity or a world history, but the record of the covenant family, told from inside the covenant family, for those being gathered home.

One phrase in this book needs orienting before the reader meets it. “Son of man” — in Hebrew “ben adam” — means son of Adam. It is a seed-designation, marking a human as of the Adamic lineage. Scripture uses it in two registers. In the creaturely register it addresses a righteous prophet: Ezekiel hears it ninety-three times; Enoch hears it in 60:10 and again in 71:14 as the Head of Days confirms his son-of-Adam lineage. In the prophetic register it names the Messiah — the Formed of the Formless coming to walk as a son of Adam — in the Parables’ visions of the Elect One (chapters 46, 48, 62, 63, 69:30–33), in Daniel 7:13, and in Yahusha’s (Jesus’s) own self-designation throughout the Gospels. Yahusha (Jesus) was quoting Enoch’s usage. Enoch is the only other text that calls the Messiah by that phrase, which is what gives the Gospels’ son-of-man sayings their Christological depth. Read both registers in the Parables as they appear. The distinction holds through Chapter 71, where Enoch is addressed in the creaturely register as the son of Adam he was, while the Elect One — who has been the son of man throughout the Parables — remains seated on the throne of glory.
