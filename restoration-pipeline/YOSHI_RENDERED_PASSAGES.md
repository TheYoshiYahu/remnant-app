# Yoshi-Rendered Passages Registry

A specific class of passages where the KJV (or whatever public-domain English base we're using) is demonstrably wrong — collapses two distinct Greek or Hebrew words into one English word, mis-renders a key technical term, or otherwise loses the meaning of the source. These passages do NOT pass through the standard modernize-and-restore pipeline. They get **Yoshi-direct-from-source renderings**, each with a footnote explaining what the underlying language actually says and why the standard English misleads.

This registry is the working list. Every entry needs:

1. **The reference** — book, chapter, verse(s).
2. **The standard reading** — what the KJV (or our base) says, and how that reading is normally understood.
3. **The problem** — what the source language actually does that the English flattens.
4. **The Yoshi rendering** — our own English, never copied from another Restored Names Edition.
5. **The footnote** — the apologetic for the rendering, in plain language, that goes inline in the app so any reader questioning the choice gets the answer on the same screen.

**Critical rule on attribution and plagiarism.** Where another Restored Names Edition (Cepher, ISR's Scriptures, Halleluyah Scriptures, etc.) has caught the same problem and rendered it correctly, that is convergence on what the source actually says — not borrowing. We do the same theological move using OUR OWN English derived directly from the source-language analysis. We never copy another publisher's English. The footnote may acknowledge that other Restored Names Editions have made the same correction (this is honest scholarship), but the rendering itself must be ours.

### 1 Corinthians 11 (entire chapter) — LANDED 2026-05-11

**Reference:** 1 Corinthians 11:1-34. The Yoshi rendering covers the whole chapter — the head-covering passage (v 1-16) and the supper passage (v 17-34) — because both carry lexical and contextual errors in the standard English that require correction at the verse level, not by regex substitution.

**Standard reading and the inherited contradictions.**

The standard English of this chapter carries five distinct errors that compound into the inherited Christian misreading of the entire passage:

1. **Two Greek words flattened to one English word in the head-covering section.** Verses 4-7, 13 use the verb *katakalúptō* (κατακαλύπτω) — *to cover down, to veil thoroughly with cloth.* Verse 15 uses the noun *peribólaion* (περιβόλαιον) — *a wrap thrown around, a mantle, a natural endowment.* The KJV translates both as *covering.* This flattening produces the absurdity at verse 15: *the woman's hair has been given to her as a covering — therefore she must cover her covering.* The standard reading swallows the absurdity; the Greek does not contain it.

2. **An interpolated noun in verse 4.** The Greek *katà kephalês échōn* literally means *having down [from] head* — with no noun object. The KJV inserted *covered* to supply an object the Greek does not provide. The Hebrew/Aramaic biblical idiom of the bowed or covered head as the shame/mourning posture (2 Samuel 15:30, Esther 6:12, Jeremiah 14:4) supplies the actual reference: the man's own head is what is cast down, in shame at his natural state, dishonoring his consecration to Messiah (Christ).

3. **A buried tail-clause equation in verse 5.** The Greek *hen gár estin kaì tò autò têi exyrēménēi* — *for it is one and the same as the shaven* — defines the *uncovered* state of the front clause as the shaven state. Uncovered IS shaven; they are not two similar things compared but one state described two ways. The flat English leaves *uncovered* undefined and lets the reader fill it with *no cloth on hair* — the very reading the tail clause forecloses.

4. **A contradiction with Torah created by verse 7.** Read flat, verse 7 forbids men from covering their heads with cloth during worship. But Torah commands Aaronic priests to wear turbans during priestly service (Exodus 28:4, Leviticus 8:9, Ezekiel 44:18). The flat reading puts Paul against Torah and fails the 1 John 2:3-4 test. The reading is therefore false. Paul is forbidding shame-covering, not cloth itself.

5. **Loaded English in the supper passage.** *New testament* in verse 25 (for *diathēkē* = covenant). *Damnation* in verse 29 (for *kríma* = judgment, decision, verdict — not eternal hellfire). *Heresies* in verse 19 (for *haíresis* = factions, sects, divisions — not the later doctrinal-trial sense). *Church* throughout (for *ekklēsía* = called-out assembly — not the institutional building). Each of these carries inherited Christian doctrinal weight the underlying Greek does not.

**The problem, in one sentence.** The standard English of 1 Corinthians 11 reads as a worship-rule on women's headgear that contradicts Torah, contradicts itself at verse 15, and obscures the consecration framework Paul establishes at verse 3 — when the Greek of the chapter, read with the Hebrew shame-idiom and the Nazarite-vow context (Acts 18:18, Numbers 6) active, reads as Paul's instruction to a Corinthian community he himself led into Nazarite practice, on how to handle the post-vow shaving the consecration cycle produced.

**The Nazarite contextual key.** Paul shaved his head at Cenchrea — the port of Corinth — at the close of his Corinthian ministry, having completed a Nazarite vow (Acts 18:18). The Corinthian believers personally witnessed the vow and its closing shave. Some among them took vows themselves following his example. Numbers 6:2 explicitly opens the Nazarite vow to both men and women; Numbers 6:5 requires uncut hair during the vow; Numbers 6:18 requires the shaving of the head at the door of the tabernacle and the burning of the hair on the altar at the close. For a woman, the post-vow shaven state is the temporary shame-state requiring cloth remediation until her natural covering grows back. This is the practical occasion of 1 Corinthians 11:4-15. Paul is not regulating women's headgear in worship generally; he is answering the practical question of how the post-vow shorn woman handles her time in the assembly until her hair returns.

**Priscilla as the witnessed female example.** Paul did not travel to Cenchrea alone. The Greek of Acts 18:18 names Priscilla and Aquila as the companions sailing with him from Corinth to Syria. The framework reading of upper-position consecration — leadership and teaching in the apostolic community walking under Nazarite vow as a standing pattern — places Priscilla and Aquila as co-vow-bearers with Paul, not as bystanders to his consecration. The thirteen-breadcrumb case across their six canonical mentions (Acts 18:2, 18:18, 18:26; Romans 16:3-5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; 2 Timothy 4:19) sustains this: covenant-lineage Yahudim, Priscilla named first in three of six mentions, joint upper-position instruction of Apollos who was already *mighty in the scriptures,* Paul's word *synergoí* (apostolic co-workers) applied to them by name, life-risking commitment (*laid down their own necks*), empire-wide recognition (*all the churches of the Gentiles*), assembly-hosting in both Rome and Ephesus across roughly fifteen years, and their occupation as *skēnopoíoi* — tabernacle-builders, the same Greek word that names the wilderness Tabernacle of Mosheh (Moses) and the heavenly *skēnē* where Yahusha (Jesus) serves as priest (the inherited Christian commentary tradition flattens *skēnopoíos* to "leather-tent tradesperson," but the Greek word itself sits in the Tabernacle lexical family, not the trade-supply one). When the Corinthian believers later asked Paul about how the post-vow shorn woman should handle her time in the assembly, they were asking about Priscilla — the woman they had personally watched walk this exact case. Verse 6 of this chapter codifies Priscilla's example: *the woman shorn yet wholly covered standeth not in disgrace; her covering is upon her.* The cloth remediates the temporary post-vow state; the woman is not in disgrace while so covered; her hair grows back; the cycle closes. The chapter is the apostolic instruction on the consecration cycle that the assembly's own teaching couple modeled.

**Footnote (LANDED — chapter-level, displayed at the end of the chapter in the app):**

> *This rendering departs from the standard English at multiple points because the standard English carries inherited errors at the lexical, idiomatic, and contextual layers. (1) The Greek of verses 4-7 and 13 uses katakalúptō (cloth covering) while verse 15 uses peribólaion (natural wrap, the hair itself). Standard English flattens both to "covering," producing the apparent contradiction that a woman should cover the hair she has been given as a covering. The two Greek words are kept distinct in this rendering: the hair is the natural covering, and cloth is the remediation when the natural covering is absent on the shorn or shaven woman. (2) Verse 4's "head cast down" tracks the Greek katà kephalês échōn (literally, "having down from head") read through the Hebrew biblical idiom of the bowed or covered head as the shame/mourning posture (2 Samuel 15:30, Esther 6:12, Jeremiah 14:4). The standard KJV inserted the word "covered" to supply a noun the Greek does not provide; this rendering does not insert one. (3) Verse 5's "the dishonor is upon her that is shaven" reflects the Greek tail clause that equates the uncovered state with the shaven state. The disgrace falls on the shorn or shaven woman who has not remediated with cloth; it does not fall on the woman whose hair is intact. (4) Verse 7's "out of shame" reflects the framework reading that what is forbidden is the shame-covering of a man embarrassed by his natural state, not cloth coverings on men's heads generally — which would contradict Torah's command for Aaronic priests to wear turbans (Exodus 28, Leviticus 8, Ezekiel 44). (5) Verse 10's "angels" preserves the specific reference to the Genesis 6 / Enoch tradition of the watchers — the angelic class whose boundary-crossing in the days of Noah makes the woman's natural covering a marker of her belonging within Yahuah's covenant order. (6) The supper passage restorations: "factions" (Greek haíresis, v 19) rather than the later doctrinal-trial sense "heresies"; "new covenant" (Greek diathēkē, v 25) rather than "new testament" (which now names a separate body of literature in English); "judgment" (Greek kríma, v 29) rather than "damnation" (which exceeds the Greek's force). The contextual key to the whole chapter: Paul completed a Nazarite vow at Cenchrea, the port of Corinth, at the close of his Corinthian ministry (Acts 18:18). The Corinthian community witnessed the vow and its closing shave. Numbers 6 opens the Nazarite vow to both sexes and requires the shaving of the head at the vow's completion. For a woman completing a vow, the post-shaving period is the temporary shame-state that the cloth remediation of verse 6 addresses. The chapter is Paul's pastoral instruction to a community he led into Nazarite practice, on how to handle the consecration cycle in the assembly — not a worship-rule about women's headgear generally.*

### Psalm 81:3 — the dark / concealed moon

**Reference:** Psalm 81:3 (and downstream feast-calendar passages affected by the same Hebrew word).

**Standard reading.** The KJV reads: *Blow up the trumpet in the new moon, in the time appointed, on our solemn feast day.* The Hebrew word translated *time appointed* is *keseh* (כֶּסֶה). Translators across English Bibles render it variously as *full moon, new moon, appointed time, solemn feast,* etc., based on context-guessing rather than the word's actual root meaning.

**The problem.** *Keseh* shares its root with *kasah* (כָּסָה), meaning *to cover, to conceal.* The literal force of *keseh* is *the concealed thing* or *the covering* — and applied to the moon, it means *the moon when it is concealed* — the dark moon at astronomical conjunction (when no moon is visible at all), not the first visible sliver that later rabbinic Judaism uses to begin the month.

This matters for the entire feast calendar. If the new moon is reckoned at the dark moon (concealment), the feast cycle starts ~1-2 days earlier than rabbinic reckoning would have it. Yoshi's framework holds the dark moon as the correct reckoning per the Hebrew root.

**Yoshi rendering (DRAFT):**

For Psalm 81:3, render something like: *Blow up the trumpet at the new moon, at the moon's concealment, on our solemn feast day.* The doubled phrase (*new moon* and *moon's concealment*) preserves both the standard understanding of *new moon* as a temporal marker and the literal force of *keseh* as the concealment that defines which day the new moon falls on.

**Footnote (DRAFT):**

> *The Hebrew word here, keseh, shares its root with kasah, "to cover, to conceal." The literal force is "the concealed moon" — the dark moon at astronomical conjunction, when no moon is visible. Translators have rendered this variously as "new moon," "full moon," or "appointed time" based on context-guessing, but the underlying word names the concealment itself. This matters for how the new month is reckoned: at the dark moon (concealment) versus at the first visible sliver. The Hebrew root favors concealment. Other Restored Names Editions have noted the same.*

**Status:** DRAFT — pending Yoshi's review of the specific English wording before it lands in the pipeline.

### Melchizedek → Melek Tsadiq (every occurrence) — LANDED

**Reference:** Every mention of *Melchizedek* in scripture and commentary. Genesis 14:18-20, Psalm 110:4, Hebrews 5:6, 5:10, 6:20, 7:1-21.

**Standard reading.** The KJV and virtually every English translation render the Hebrew as *Melchizedek* — one English compound word, treated as a proper name. Most English readers receive it as a proper noun with no transparent meaning, the way they receive *Abraham* or *Isaac.*

**The problem.** *Melchizedek* is not one word. It is two: **מלך** (*melek* — king) + **צדק** (*tsadiq* — righteousness, righteous). The Hebrew literally reads *king of righteousness* or *my king is righteousness.* The collapsing of two Hebrew words into one English compound buries the meaning the name itself carries — and the meaning matters, because Hebrews 7 explicitly unpacks the name (*first being by interpretation King of righteousness, and after that also King of Salem, which is, King of peace*) and treats the meaning as theologically load-bearing for the whole argument about Yahusha (Jesus) being a priest after the order of Melek Tsadiq.

**Yoshi rendering — LANDED.**

Render every occurrence of *Melchizedek* as **Melek Tsadiq (Melchizedek)** on every mention, every time, in body text and in scripture quotations both — same standard as the deity and covenant-people names (Yahuah (God), Yahusha (Jesus), Yashar'el (Israel), Yahudah (Judah)). Two Hebrew words, parenthetical English equivalent every time. The rendering is not a Yoshi-specific judgment call requiring per-passage approval; it's a sacred-name restoration that applies universally and gets enforced by the deterministic restoration pipeline.

**Footnote (LANDED — placed at the first occurrence of Melek Tsadiq in any book that uses the name):**

> *The Hebrew name rendered Melchizedek in most English translations is in fact two words: melek (king) and tsadiq (righteousness). The literal meaning is "king of righteousness" — which Hebrews 7:2 explicitly unpacks. The English compound buries the meaning the name carries. We render the two Hebrew words distinctly so the title's force is preserved.*

**Status:** LANDED. Apply universally. Pipeline-enforced. No per-passage Yoshi review needed — this is a standing restoration rule, not a per-verse judgment call.

## Pipeline Integration

The deterministic restoration pipeline (Phase 3) loads this registry at runtime and, when processing a verse that matches a registered reference, substitutes the Yoshi rendering for the modernize-and-restore output, attaches the footnote as inline metadata on the verse, and marks the verse's `source_type` field as `"yoshi-rendering"` instead of `"restored-names-kjv"`.

In the app's UI (Phase 5/6), verses with `source_type == "yoshi-rendering"` get a small visual indicator (a footnote marker, or a colored asterisk, or similar — UI design TBD) and the footnote is one tap away. The reader sees the rendering, sees that it differs from the KJV, and can immediately read why on the same screen.

## Workflow For Adding A New Entry

When Yoshi flags a new passage:

1. Yoshi names the reference and the issue (the source-language word that the KJV flattens or mis-renders).
2. Claude drafts the rendering and the footnote.
3. Yoshi reviews and either approves or rewrites.
4. Once approved, the entry moves from DRAFT to LANDED status here.
5. The pipeline picks up the LANDED entries on its next run.
6. The verse appears in the parsed JSON with `source_type: yoshi-rendering` and the footnote attached.
