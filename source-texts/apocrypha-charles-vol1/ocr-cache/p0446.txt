XXXII. 14—XXXIII. 31 (=G& XXXV. 14—XXXVI. 16@ and XXX. 25-40). Here a new section of the book
begins. The main themes that emerge in it are: (1) Wisdom above all else consists in foresight against danger. The
imprudence of the ungodly man is the result of pride, and the blindness so engendered ; the pious man, on the other
hand, finds the right path by study of the Law and attention to its teaching (xxxil. 14—xxxiil. 6 = @& xxxv. 14—xxxvi. 6):
(2) a justification of the apparent arbitrariness of providential choice (xxxiii. 7-15 = Q& xxxvi. 7-15); (3) an appeal to
the rulers and guides of the people to listen (xxxiii, 16-18 = Gh xxxvi. 16a, xxx. 25-27). All this, as Smend points out,
is introductory to the teaching that follows on (a) the importance of maintaining independence (xxxiii. 19-23 = Q& xxx.
28-32); and (4) on keeping slaves and subordinates in order (xxxiii. 24-31 = G xxx. 33-40).
